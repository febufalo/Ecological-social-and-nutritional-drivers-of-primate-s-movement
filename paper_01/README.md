Feeding ecology and cognitive flexibility shape route planning across Central and South American primates

Data availability: Raw data are stored in a private repository and are available upon request to the authors.

Abstract: Primates, with their ecological diversity, social complexity, and advanced cognition, provide powerful models for investigating the mechanisms underlying movement decisions in complex environments. Yet, despite strong evidence that resource distribution shapes primate travel routes, little is known about how different dietary strategies drive variation in route-planning behavior. Here, we compare how Central and South American primates, with distinct dietary strategies, plan their daily routes in response to seasonality, type, and spatial distribution of feeding resources. Using ranging and feeding data from 14 species across Central and South America, we found that clumped and predictable resources—such as fruits and leaves—served as the main drivers of route planning and were associated with the most linear daily paths among feeding guilds. Seasonal variation also modulated movement patterns: during periods of high resource availability, folivores exhibited greater daily route linearity and stronger orientation toward feeding patches, whereas under resource scarcity, feeding guilds converged in movement characteristics, suggesting reduced planning effort and increased opportunism to reach less diverse feeding resources. Overall, our results reveal that primate route planning emerges from the interplay between feeding ecology, spatial predictability, and temporal variability of resources. These dynamics highlight that spatial cognition in primates is not a fixed trait but a flexible strategy—tuned to ecological context and resource uncertainty—that allows species to balance efficiency, exploration, and energetic demands in changing environments.
