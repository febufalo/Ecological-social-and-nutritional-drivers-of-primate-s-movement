Social and environmental drivers of route planning in an Endangered primate

Data availability: raw data are stored in a private repository and are available upon request to the authors.

Requests for access should be sent to: febufalo@gmail.com

Abstract: Understanding how animals integrate environmental and social information during movement is essential for predicting their responses to habitat change, but analyses of the interplay between memory, habitat features, and social dynamics are very rare. Here, we provide the first integrated assessment of how habitat features and resource distribution, spatial memory, and the social landscape and predation risk jointly shape route planning, using data from the Endangered black lion tamarin (BLT, Leontopithecus chrysopygus), an arboreal primate. In a 100-ha seasonal Atlantic Forest fragment where three groups coexist, we combined high-resolution GPS and accelerometer data with spatially explicit models of food availability, canopy structure, energetic expenditure, and high-resolution vocal activity data, collected across rainy and dry seasons. Integrated step-selection models revealed that, in both seasons, BLTs selected taller canopies and areas with higher densities of previously known fruiting trees, indicating a synergistic role of spatial memory and habitat structure on habitat use. Rainy-season movement was additionally modulated by energetic expenditure, whereas dry-season movements were shaped by elevated risk perception, with alarm calls driving shorter, more tortuous paths. Long calls affected movement differently across seasons, reflecting shifting social pressures. These results show that BLTs flexibly integrate spatial, energetic, and social cues to navigate complex forests, highlighting how route planning emerges from the interplay between memory, habitat features, and the social landscape.
